## 1. Overview of Jung's Theory

### 1.1 Introduction to Carl Jung

Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961) was a Swiss psychologist and the founder of Analytical Psychology. He was once a student and collaborator of Freud, and later developed his own psychological system independently because of their theoretical disagreements.

### 1.2 The core theoretical framework

#### 1.2.1 The theory of the structure of the personality

Jung divided the personality into three layers:

Consciousness (Ego)

- The part of ourselves that we recognize in everyday self-awareness
- Like a beam of light, it illuminates a small part of the psyche
- It contains the thoughts, feelings, and memories we are able to bring to awareness
Personal Unconscious
- The personal realm that lies beneath consciousness, it forms the hidden substratum of the human psychic structure, like a seed bank buried deep in the soil of memory. This realm is not wholly chaotic but has clear layers and order, and it carries the unique imprint of an individual life.
- It is made up of material that was once conscious but was later repressed, forgotten, or ignored: traumatic experiences from childhood, desires suppressed by social norms, memory fragments deliberately forgotten out of fear, and so on. These contents do not truly vanish; they go on shaping an individual's cognition, emotion, and behavior in unconscious form.
- Its basic unit is the complex, which Jung called "the royal road to the unconscious." Complexes work like psychic magnets, drawing in the memories, feelings, and thoughts related to them and forming highly concentrated nodes of psychic energy. By analyzing dreams, slips of the tongue, artistic creation, and similar phenomena, psychologists can catch the traces of a complex at work.
- A complex is like a small personality with energy of its own: it not only has its own emotional tone and behavioral tendencies, but can also take over an individual's conscious control in certain situations. A person with a strong power complex, for example, may unconsciously seek psychological satisfaction by controlling others, even when such behavior contradicts his own rational understanding.
Collective Unconscious  Jung's most revolutionary and original theoretical discovery in the field of psychoanalysis, it broke through the limits of Freud's individual unconscious. The concept refers to the universal psychic structures that humanity has sedimented over the course of its evolution as a species; transcending individual experience, cultural difference, and the boundaries of time and space, they exist in the depths of the collective unconscious in the form of shared archetypes.
- Universal archetypal structures shared across cultures, eras, and peoples  Archaeological findings and cross-cultural research confirm that the archetypes of the collective unconscious recur again and again, from ancient Greek myth to Eastern yin-yang philosophy, from tribal totems to modern science fiction. The "mother archetype," for instance, appears both in the loving image of the Virgin Mary and in the educational wisdom of Mencius's mother moving house three times in Eastern culture; the "hero archetype" runs through the heroic narratives of different civilizations, from Heracles to Sun Wukong.
- Not acquired by the individual but inherited as a universal human psychic structure  Jung held that the collective unconscious is passed down from generation to generation through heredity, engraved on the human psyche much as biological genes are. This inheritance is not gene transmission in the biological sense; rather, the psychic experience accumulated over the course of the species' evolution forms a priori psychic dispositions that shape how individuals perceive, think, and behave.
- Archetypal composition  As the core element of the collective unconscious, an archetype is a symbolic psychic pattern. Besides the familiar "shadow," "anima/animus," and "persona," these include the "wise old man," who symbolizes the transmission of knowledge; the "Great Mother," who represents the dual power of nurturing and destruction; and the "mandala," which symbolizes psychic integration. Through vehicles such as dreams, artistic creation, and religious ritual, these archetypes continue to manifest in metaphorical form in the human mind.

#### 1.2.2 Archetype theory

Archetypes are the basic elements of the collective unconscious. They appear as universal symbols and images, and recur in myth, religion, and dreams. The core archetypes include:

Shadow

- The repressed "dark side" of the personality: Jung held that the shadow consists of the parts of individual consciousness that are repressed, denied, or rejected. It contains the traits and impulses that do not fit one's idealized self-image or society's moral standards, and it is one of the deep structures of the human psyche.
- It contains primal instincts, aggression, and unintegrated desires: the shadow encompasses the primal instincts inherited from humanity's distant past, such as the survival instinct and the sexual instinct, and also the aggressive impulses that individuals repress under social discipline and the various desires denied by mainstream values as they grow up. These contents usually exist in unconscious form, waiting to be discovered and accepted.
- An unintegrated shadow drains psychic energy: when the shadow remains unnoticed and unintegrated for a long time, it consumes a great deal of psychic energy. The individual must keep investing effort in defense and repression, which impedes personal growth and can even give rise to anxiety, depression, and other psychological problems, hindering the complete and harmonious development of the personality.
Persona
- Derived from the Latin word for an actor's mask, the persona was defined by Jung as the public image an individual carefully constructs in social interaction; it is the medium and the buffer zone between us and the outside world
- By selectively presenting traits that fit social norms, cultural expectations, or the demands of a particular situation—a professional image at work, say, or an approachable manner at a social gathering—this psychic mechanism helps individuals fit smoothly into group life
- When individuals rely too heavily on the persona and equate it with their true self, the phenomenon of the "mask devouring the personality" may arise, leading to suppressed inner feeling, distorted self-perception, and even an identity crisis
Anima and Animus
- The anima in the male psyche and the animus in the female psyche are among the most representative archetypal images in Jungian analytical psychology. The anima symbolizes the tender, emotional, and mysterious feminine traits in a man's unconscious, while the animus represents the decisive, rational, and forceful masculine features in a woman's unconscious.
- These archetypal images profoundly shape how an individual perceives the opposite sex and how intimate relationships are built. When the anima or animus is projected onto a real partner, it may give rise to idealized romantic attraction, or it may lead to emotional conflict when reality falls short of the ideal. A healthy process of projection helps individuals complete their self-integration within the relationship; otherwise it can produce cognitive distortion and emotional exhaustion.
- As a psychic "cross-gender" medium, the anima and animus are a bridge to deep mutual understanding between men and women. They break through the barriers of biological sex and enable individuals to sense the psychological needs and emotional patterns of the opposite sex at an unconscious level, prompting both sides toward a more inclusive and more profound dialogue of the soul in intimate relationships, and thus fostering psychological resonance and mutual growth between the sexes.
- 
Self
- As the core concept of Jungian analytical psychology, the Self is the ultimate symbol of psychic wholeness. It transcends the opposition between consciousness and the unconscious and unites all the opposing and contradictory aspects of an individual personality
- In artistic creation and dreams, the Self is often made concrete in the form of the mandala. Through its precise symmetrical structure, this circular geometric pattern mirrors the process by which inner psychic order is rebuilt
- The Self represents the wholeness and unity of the personality, covering not only the traits an individual already knows but also integrating such deep psychic elements as the repressed shadow and the archetypal images of the collective unconscious
- It is not only the ultimate goal of psychological development but also the core driving force of the individuation process. Through connection with the Self, individuals can complete the transformation from the ego to a whole personality, attaining harmony and transcendence on the spiritual level

#### 1.2.3 The theory of psychic dynamics

- The psyche is a relatively closed system, or a self-sufficient energy system. Jung held that the psyche has a distinctive autonomy: it does not depend on a continuous supply of energy from the external environment, but instead circulates and transforms energy through its own internal psychic activity. This closure is not absolute isolation; rather, it emphasizes that the psyche possesses the capacity for self-regulation and energy regeneration as it maintains its own operation and development, like a finely tuned ecosystem in which the various psychic elements depend on and interact with one another.
- Psychic energy is the motive force of the personality, and it may be either conscious or unconscious. Jung regarded psychic energy as the core source of drive behind an individual's thoughts, feelings, and behavior, running through the whole course of personality development. On the conscious level, psychic energy appears as the drive to think, decide, and act; in the unconscious, it lies hidden in dreams, intuitions, and unconscious impulses, shaping mental activity of which we are unaware. A person's intense passion for a cause, for example, may stem either from a clearly conscious pursuit of a goal or from energy aroused by unnoticed childhood experiences or deep psychological needs.
- Psychic energy influences mental activity through transformation. Psychic energy does not vanish into nothing; it changes form as it passes among different psychic structures and activities. When individuals repress an emotion, that portion of psychic energy does not dissipate but may be converted into physical symptoms or substitute behaviors. People who excessively suppress anger, for instance, may develop migraines or other bodily complaints, or release the repressed energy through unconscious aggressive behavior. This mechanism of energy transformation profoundly affects an individual's psychological balance and behavioral expression.
- Jung borrowed the principle of energy conservation from physics to explain the transfer of psychic energy. Drawing on the law of conservation of energy, he proposed that within the psychic system the total quantity of energy remains relatively stable, so that a strengthening of one psychic activity must be accompanied by a weakening of another. When individuals invest a large amount of psychic energy in the pursuit of achievement at work, for example, they may correspondingly reduce their investment in family relationships or personal interests. This theory offers a scientific analogical framework for understanding complex human mental activity and behavior, revealing the dynamic equilibrium of psychic energy among different psychic activities and personality structures.

#### 1.2.4 The individuation process

- Psychological development is essentially a process of individuation, which Jung saw as a lifelong quest of the psyche. In this process, individuals must actively explore the deep connections between consciousness and the unconscious and, by facing the shadow and accepting such archetypal images as the anima and animus, gradually achieve a dynamic integration of the ego and the collective unconscious.
- When individuation advances effectively, the parts of the personality begin to emerge from fragmentation and conflict; the conscious ego no longer clings to a one-sided social mask (the persona) but comes to terms with the wisdom of the unconscious, eventually forming a personality structure with a centering force and attaining wholeness and inner harmony.
- This integrative process is one of the central goals of Jungian analytical psychology. It goes beyond simple standards of mental health and points to the ultimate possibilities of human spiritual development. Jung emphasized that only those who complete individuation can truly become "individuated beings" and attain full psychological maturity.
- The ultimate significance of individuation lies in helping individuals break through the collective molding of society and culture and shake off the shackles of other people's expectations, so that they become truly independent and self-sufficient spiritual subjects. By confronting the primordial images and deep drives within, individuals can build a value system of their own and achieve genuine self-definition and self-transcendence on the existential level.

## 2. The Jungian Eight Cognitive Functions

### 2.1 Theoretical basis

The Jungian eight functions (the theory of psychological types) is a model of cognitive functions proposed by Jung in Psychological Types and later formalized into a model by scholars such as Katharine Briggs and her daughter Isabel Myers and John Beebe.

### 2.2 Basic composition

Jung distinguished two basic attitudes and four psychological functions; combining them in pairs yields the eight functions:

#### 2.2.1 The two basic attitudes

Extraversion (E)

- 
- Energy comes from the outer world: extraverts obtain and replenish psychic energy mainly through interaction with the external environment. In rich and varied settings they tend to activate their thinking quickly and generate an inexhaustible vitality. This pattern of energy flow makes them more adaptable to changes in the external environment and gives them a strong ability to blend in.
- Preference for social interaction and action: they are eager to take part in all kinds of social activities, proving their worth through group exchange and cooperation and striking sparks of ideas off other people. At the same time, they tend to put ideas into action quickly, testing their notions through practical operation; rather than deliberating at length, they prefer to explore and adjust in practice.
- Attention to the external environment and to other people: such individuals are highly sensitive to changes in their surroundings and quickly pick up on others' emotional shifts and needs as well as on potential opportunities and challenges in the environment. In interpersonal dealings they give priority to others' opinions and feelings, are good at coordinating relationships among the parties involved, and push collective goals forward
- 
Introversion (I)
- Energy comes from the inner world: introverts restore their energy through solitude, meditation, reading, and other deeply inward activities. When they are in social situations or exposed to too much external stimulation, they tire easily and need to retreat into their own inner world to "recharge."
- Preference for reflection and solitude: they are more inclined to think through and explore problems on their own, using solitude for inner dialogue, for analyzing their experiences and drawing conclusions from them. Compared with exchanging ideas with others, they take more pleasure in weighing an idea over and over in their own mental space.
- Attention to inner experience and ideas: they care more about their own subjective feelings, emotional shifts, and thought processes, and their perception and judgment of external events is usually filtered through an inner value system. External reality moves them only after it has been translated into inner experience.

#### 2.2.2 The four psychological functions

Sensation (S)

- Obtains information through sensory perception, taking in the world through keen sight, hearing, and touch, and converting concrete elements such as shifts of light and shadow, fluctuations of sound, and textures into units of information the brain can process
- Focuses on actual sensations and experience, tending to derive patterns from firsthand experience and concrete events and to trust the information conveyed by scenes it has touched, observed, and lived through
- Attends to concrete reality and details, focusing on how things present themselves in the moment and never missing the fine grain of an object or a small turn in the course of events, building an understanding of the whole through a precise grasp of the parts
Intuition (N)
- 
- Does not rely on sensory perception but gathers information through a subconscious "sixth sense." This non-concrete mode of receiving information often appears as sudden inspiration, intuitive judgment, or symbolic revelation in dreams, breaking free of the constraints of practical experience and physical law
- Attends to the patterns behind phenomena, the hidden connections among things, and the potential possibilities of future development. Rather than focusing only on present concrete details, it is adept at catching the direction in which things are moving and building a holistic framework of understanding from fragmentary clues
- Is eager to explore the latent meaning of events and symbols, turning surface appearances into deep psychological images. Through metaphor, symbol, and archetype, it excavates the collective unconscious content hidden beneath everyday experience and reveals the essence of things and their deep connections

Thinking (T)

- 
- Makes decisions on the basis of logic and objective analysis, using systematic reasoning together with verifiable facts and data, and excluding the interference of subjective emotion so that every decision stands up to rational scrutiny.
- Pursues external efficiency, objective logic, and control of results; it emphasizes optimizing process design to reduce the waste of resources, guarantees the predictability of decisions by establishing clear chains of cause and effect, and uses quantitative indicators to track progress toward goals in real time.
- Builds systems that are logically self-consistent, forming a closed architecture that runs from basic concepts to applied methodology, in which each element supports the others and builds level by level, so that the whole theoretical or practical framework maintains inner consistency and stability as it develops.

Feeling (F)

- 
- Evaluates things on the basis of personal values and emotional responses, usually taking a deep inner value system as its yardstick: when facing a choice or a judgment, it first considers whether something accords with one's own moral principles, aesthetic preferences, and emotional resonance.
- Attends to group harmony and shared moral values, constantly watching the atmosphere within a group and the relationships among its members and actively maintaining a harmonious collective environment. When opinions diverge in a team, it works to reconcile the various positions and seeks solutions that serve the interests of the majority, while strictly observing and advocating mainstream social morality and steering the group in a positive and healthy direction.
- Is skilled at empathy and at preserving harmony; with keen emotional perception it quickly picks up changes in others' feelings and puts itself in their place. Whether a friend is unburdening a worry or a colleague is under pressure, it listens patiently, offers comfort, and acts as a "lubricant" in interpersonal dealings, dissolving conflict, preventing escalation, and striving to create a harmonious and friendly social environment.

### 2.3 The eight cognitive functions in detail

#### 2.3.1 Extraverted Sensation (Se)

Accumulates "actual experience" of concrete objects, approaching everything in the outer world enthusiastically as a significant other.

1. Sensitive to sensory cues: compulsively attends to particular sensory details (sight, hearing, touch, smell...); is consciously aware of its own spatial position and of the spatial position of other objects; can detect interrelated facts within a mass of data.
1. Eager for improvisation: easily and freely follows physical impulses or instincts as they arise; immediately enjoys the pleasant sensation produced by bodily movement (as in sports); can readily coordinate with the bodily movements of other people or objects to form a smooth, linked action.
1. Merges with the immediate environment: all useful knowledge and skills are learned from the tangible environment around it; almost every day it unhurriedly does something requiring hands-on work (crafts); when perceiving things around it (sight, hearing, touch, smell...), it sometimes becomes so absorbed as to lose itself.
1. Acts quickly: takes action fast to obtain immediate results; rapidly checks the available cues to judge how far it can solve the problem; can change its actions freely according to the moment, trying to maximize the gain or the "spoils" as much as possible.
1. A striking personality: always has some clever trick that produces a stunning effect and draws a response from others; in taking action it uses unconventional techniques to achieve startling results beyond expectation; sometimes it works itself to the bone on something simply to attract other people's attention.

#### 2.3.2 Introverted Sensation (Si)

Moved by the "subjective sensation" evoked by objective stimuli, it uses observations drawn from the experience of others to reinforce the experience of the self.

1. Sensitive to detailed data: habitually collects detailed information; can identify very subtle perceptual objects (usually familiar ones), such as a particular smell or a particular way of spelling something; compulsively suppresses its urge to try new experiences.
1. Trusts common knowledge: feels relaxed in body and mind and recovers its energy when taking part in traditional, customary activities; naturally follows the ideas and examples of those who came first (who have already put them into practice); is used to doing routine work or activities at a steady pace every day.
1. Carefully compares past and present: can recall the former circumstances or forms of events and objects with precise detail; can point out the differences between how things used to be compared and how they are compared now; is keenly aware of when something before it matches expectations on a particular detail.
1. Builds a basis of trust for itself: collects a great deal of information over the long term to affirm or confirm that an existing standard is correct; reviews past experience to draw lessons from it and reach conclusions of "hindsight"; compares information and data with its own store of knowledge and experience, extracting the components it deems "trustworthy."
1. Upholds tradition no matter how old it is: patiently educates others, training them to fit the social roles they "ought" to occupy and guiding them in handling the "new" problems they have not met before in life; upholds civilized cultivation and protects what is known and long established.
1. Can reach both past and future: memory is transformed through language and revised by the mind; it can achieve self-satisfaction through subjective sensation in response to objective stimuli; it is a type much given to "dreaming." At a high level of development it brings an artistic "romantic" quality; poorly developed, it produces self-deception.

#### 2.3.3 Extraverted Intuition (Ne)

Becomes involved in the possibilities of the object and submerged in new situations, and thereby gains full satisfaction. It moves from object to abstraction and associates upon the abstracted result; the association may retain only the faintest connection to the original information, and it can leap from result to result, using association to make one jump after another.

1. "Hidden meanings" are obscure: can "sense" synchronic moments; can grasp the analogies and metaphors used to explain a point of view; experiences inexplicable moments of "knowing" or other uncanny instants.
1. Enjoys free association: values free debate and trusts the conclusions reached in it; associates utterly unrelated ideas just to see what they will finally combine into; can pluck a single viewpoint at will from a string of ideas (floating, as it were, in the air).
1. Catches the stream of creative consciousness: feels an urge to act at once on possibilities as they occur; lets thought leap back and forth among many intangible notions and abstract ideas without fixing on any one of them; can "sense" almost infinitely many possible meanings in a string of ideas.
1. What it says must astonish: can transfer perspectives or views from other situations into the present one at random; on first encountering a viewpoint, quickly thinks of a whole set of possible accounts of it; delights in imaginative scenario play, freely combining possibilities.
1. Catalyzes possibility into reality: "catalyzes" other people and now and then improvises changes to the environment; brings about change in the environment and in others through indirect suggestion or an indirect leadership style; spreads an atmosphere of change in the role of an emergent leader.

#### 2.3.4 Introverted Intuition (Ni)

Attends to the background processes of consciousness, understanding the images that well up from the collective unconscious (the innately inherited unconscious) and linking subjective images to actual situations. It moves from abstraction to object or from abstraction to abstraction, subsuming abstracted results under similar cases in order to arrive at what they share, or relying on what similar cases share to reach a result—"seeing autumn in a single falling leaf."

1. Insight: has experienced a foreknowledge of things beyond expectation (precognition); is compulsively drawn to symbolic things, to things pertaining to "archetypes," or to things with a mystical coloring; without warning and out of nowhere, suddenly "sees" the answer to something.
1. Believes in future change: feels greatly excited when absorbed in envisioning future change or transformation; works persistently to realize the vision (dream) of the change that ought to come; is convinced that the change foreseen today will indeed occur in the future.
1. A mystical spiritual guide: relies on chanting, incantation, or other symbolic acts to foretell, inspire, or transform; can gain profound realization from spiritual purification or a certain mystical state; reveals "what will happen in the future" on the basis of invisible trends or omens.
1. Offers innovative answers: grasps a complex concept or system by a new approach; deliberately falls asleep with a specific question in mind and wakes with the answer; conceives a novel or symbolic way of understanding things that are universally present.
1. "Being" can "shape-shift": regularly or habitually uses certain methods to alter its way of understanding "being"; by focusing on another possible mode of its own existence, it can "transform" itself; can create an exceedingly great solution that transcends the particular problem itself.

#### 2.3.5 Extraverted Thinking (Te)

All behavior is subordinated to the conclusions of reason and always takes objectively oriented material as its ultimate means, whether that material is external fact or a generally accepted idea, elevating objective truth or an objectively oriented rational formula into a governing principle suited not only to itself but to the whole environment.

1. Sensitive to the causal order of things: keenly aware when something is not running properly; when working on a task, accurately gauges how much time remains and what step it has reached; reasons in a linear, logical fashion.
1. Trusts empirical thinking: thinks only about observable, measurable realities; when attacking another's position, uses logic both sides can follow; can give a logical explanation for its own decisions and conclusions.
1. Makes good use of control methods: adopts "sequential action" and plans an optimal allocation of time in order to achieve the greatest efficiency; suppresses impulsive behavior and isolates itself from the influence of personal feelings and emotional reactions; integrates information and ideas into a formula that is easy to carry out.
1. Establishes objective procedures: measures whether goals have been met, or success and failure, by quantitative and other objective methods; draws up contingency plans for a range of possible situations; devises "step-by-step" working procedures for detailed and complex tasks.
1. Insists on strategic management: allocates resources for macro-level, multi-module plans and oversees their execution; establishes for others a method by which goals can (successfully) be reached; organizes and supervises human and material resources to achieve high output and high efficiency.

#### 2.3.6 Introverted Thinking (Ti)

Builds a magnificent world of concepts out of vague images. It will not abandon thinking about an idea merely because that idea is dangerous, destructive, heretical, or hurtful to other people's feelings, and it investigates the truth of a theory without regard to personal likes and dislikes or positions; thus whatever contradicts its own theory and understanding it will pursue at any cost, because such things may mean that its own thinking is wrong, and it will search for the most correct theory, or the optimal solution (a universal formula).

1. Sensitive to logical categories: adept at recognizing deductive reasoning; can correctly classify and match information and data according to definitions and models; can detect logical confusion in a statement.
1. Values dialectical thinking: estimates the features of things by a model (usually) with only rough precision; concentrates on finding just the right wording to express a meaning precisely; analyzes and criticizes in order to clear away or negate whatever does not conform to a principle; always feels it does not yet understand a matter well enough to see it whole, and so is forever on the road of exploring truth.
1. Adopts a "problem-solving" mode: feels especially energetic when analyzing challenging problems or technical difficulties; tends to look for a "general method" to solve a specific problem; breaks things down to understand the principle by which they work.
1. Refines macro-level models: seeks precise wording that accurately describes the core features of a method or category; constantly seeks greater precision, clarifying subtle differences of definition among refined principles; stands outside the object of study and analyzes it from many angles and starting points.
1. Holds to a single foundational principle: can think about a problem's solution from many different angles at once; can freely draw on information and knowledge from several different fields at the same time; will adopt one foundational principle for a long period as the anchor of all its thinking, keeping thought coherent and consistent.

#### 2.3.7 Extraverted Feeling (Fe)

Shows adaptability to external situations, its feeling attuned to the objective situation and to shared values. Toward people and things it recognizes only good and bad, right and wrong, not likes and dislikes, and it distills other people's views into a value system—"right" and "wrong"—that serves as its standard for judging things; the values it forms therefore stay quite close to the prevailing views of the people around it.

1. Feels what others need: an unconscious sense of responsibility arises in its heart, a feeling that it ought to look after other people's feelings; it resonates inwardly with what others say, such as how much something matters to them; it openly accepts others' feelings and can put itself in their place.
1. Believes in "shared values": rather than hiding its feelings, it tends to disclose itself, expressing its inner feelings, value orientations, and past experiences; it endorses and is loyal to public values, public sentiment, and social norms; it is affected by other people's judgments of it, especially the judgments of those it admires and reveres.
1. Bonds through social contact: shows guests the courtesy and consideration of host to guest, making them very comfortable; gives other people's feelings and views the praise, care, and respect they deserve; responds warmly to others' compliments and expresses respect and thanks for the support it receives.
1. Expresses intimate connection: can merge emotionally with others so that they feel entirely close; can very easily take others' needs and values onto itself, as if it truly thought the same way; guides others into the social custom of "equal exchange, mutual accommodation," hoping everyone will come to see it as the natural way.
1. Spreads its values like a respected moral exemplar: can easily hold personal exchanges with anyone in a group, feeling like one of the family; to meet other people's needs, it can build close ties with many people at once.

#### 2.3.8 Introverted Feeling (Fi)

A feeling that markedly devalues the object; the values it forms are quite subjective. Its aim is not to adapt itself to the object but to go beyond it, realizing a latent image through an unconscious effort; for this reason it shows itself in a negative guise in the great majority of cases, and the existence of its positive feeling can only be inferred indirectly.

1. Clearly distinguishes good from evil: feels intensely whether something is "good" or "evil"; is clearly aware of "what I want," "what my motives are," and "what I feel inside"; can detect when a person harbors false or insincere motives.
1. Holds firmly to personal conviction: follows some absolutized ideal, person, belief system, or powerful identity; loyally supports and follows the "group" it belongs to, whatever that group does; is wholly sincere and consistent in its desires and expectations for itself and for others.
1. Makes existential choices: weighs how a conviction can be applied to itself; can choose "how I ought to feel" and, if need be, change its own feelings; constantly reflects on whether its choices accord with its inner values and deep motives.
1. Establishes inner harmony: can identify other people's real needs; when others' needs conflict with its own values, it sets up a psychic barrier to isolate the conflict, thereby protecting the needs of both sides; it silently holds to its own principles and influences others gently.
1. Maintains a humane balance: continually weighs the value of everything; patiently balances the elements of peace and conflict in every situation of life; lives by a faith grounded in a universal sense of public spirit and benevolence.

### 2.4 The order of the functions

In John Beebe's model, the eight functions are not equal in rank but are arranged in a fixed order:

Bright-side functions (positions 1-4)

- 
- These are the cognitive functions an individual actively uses in daily life. In different combinations they shape each person's distinctive information-processing patterns and behavioral style, and they are the key point of entry for understanding differences in personality.
- They constitute the core personality traits and serve as the cornerstone of the personality edifice, influencing not only an individual's decision preferences and communication style but also, over long-term development, gradually forming stable behavioral habits and psychological dispositions.

1. Dominant (Hero): the most skilled and most confident cognitive tool, almost fully internalized as an automatic response. When using this function, an individual shows extremely high efficiency and fluency, as natural as breathing, and can respond quickly and accurately to most situations in life. A dominant extraverted intuition (Ne) type, for example, shows unmatched enthusiasm and creativity in exploring new ideas and possibilities.
1. Auxiliary (Parent): a reliable backstop that supports the dominant function at the core and safeguards the dynamic balance of the personality. It compensates for the dominant function's shortcomings and helps the individual respond flexibly in more varied situations. When a dominant introverted thinking (Ti) type, for example, gets lost in excessive rational analysis, the auxiliary extraverted feeling (Fe) function can help it attend to the emotional atmosphere around it and make more humane decisions.
1. Tertiary (Eternal Child): compared with the first two, this function is less developed but holds great potential. It begins to bud in adolescence and, as life experience accumulates, can be markedly strengthened through deliberate training. It often serves as the opening through which an individual breaks out of the comfort zone and expands the dimensions of the personality, bringing fresh experience and opportunities for growth.
1. Inferior (Anima-Animus): the least mature and least manageable part of the personality structure, usually forced into use under stress or in extreme emotional states. Its way of operating often feels unfamiliar and uncomfortable to the individual, even frustrating; yet through conscious exploration and integration, the inferior function can become an important resource that awakens inner potential and brings the personality to wholeness.

Shadow functions (positions 5-8)

- As the hidden dimension of the Jungian eight-function theory, the shadow functions correspond to cognitive patterns in an individual's unconscious. Like reefs buried deep on the seabed of consciousness, they lie dormant most of the time and take no direct part in everyday decision-making or outward perception, yet they continue to shape an individual's deeper mental activity and inner value judgments.
- Shadow functions usually break through the threshold of consciousness only in situations of extreme stress (a sudden crisis or a major upheaval in life), during the healing of psychological trauma, or in the course of deep self-exploration through meditation, psychotherapy, and the like. Their activation is often accompanied by violent emotional swings and may bring either a breakthrough leap in understanding or a brief spell of self-doubt and cognitive confusion. An individual who normally relies on rational judgment may suddenly display strongly emotion-driven behavior under high pressure, for instance, or a person accustomed to extraverted socializing may fall into deep introspection after a trauma; such uncharacteristic manifestations are typical signs that the shadow functions have begun to operate.

## 3. Extensions and Developments of the Theory

### 3.1 MBTI personality theory

The MBTI is a personality assessment tool developed from Jung's theory of psychological types by Katharine Briggs and her daughter Isabel Myers. It expanded Jung's eight dominant types into the 16 types by adding the Judging–Perceiving (J/P) dimension. It should be noted that the MBTI differs from Jung's original theory: Jung's theory focuses more on psychic dynamics and the individuation process, whereas the MBTI leans more toward classification and matching at the applied level.

### 3.2 Other extensions

#### 3.2.1 The Enneagram

- Although it does not derive directly from Jung's theory, it has much in common with Jungian psychology
- Both are concerned with the wholeness and the developmental nature of personality
- Both emphasize self-knowledge and personal growth

#### 3.2.2 Type archetype theory

- A model developed by John Beebe
- It introduces functional positions and assigns an archetype to each position
- It attempts to make up for the MBTI's shortcomings in understanding the dynamics of personality

#### 3.2.3 Sakinorva theory and tools

Sakinorva is a hardcore assessment tool based on Jungian cognitive function theory, developed by researchers in the field. It departs from the MBTI's letter-based dichotomies and focuses directly on measuring the Jungian eight cognitive functions.

##### Key features

1. Cross-validation across multiple models: it uses several Jungian cognitive function scoring models, such as Grant, Myers, and Brownsword, and provides type results under different theoretical frameworks, making it suitable for theoretical verification and comparison.
1. Highly transparent data: it displays the raw scores of the eight cognitive functions directly, with no black-box processing, so users can inspect their own function scores and understand their cognitive function preferences.
1. Strongly theory-oriented: it is designed entirely on the basis of Jungian cognitive function theory, and its items revolve around how the cognitive functions operate, making it suitable for self-analysis and research by learners with a basic knowledge of Jung's theory and by theorists.

##### Limitations

- The wording of the items is fairly obscure and full of abstract theoretical concepts; it is not friendly to beginners and easily leads to misunderstanding.
- The interface is plain to the point of being crude, offering only data tables with no accessible personality descriptions or interpretations; users need some knowledge of Jungian theory to analyze the results themselves.
- The test contains a large number of items and takes considerable time to complete, and it demands a high level of self-knowledge from the test taker.

## 4. Practical Applications

### 4.1 Personal growth

- 
- Self-knowledge: the Jungian eight-function theory makes it possible to analyze systematically how one receives information (the perceiving functions) and how one reasons toward decisions (the judging functions). Individuals who prefer introverted intuition (Ni), for example, tend to discern essences from abstract concepts, whereas those dominated by extraverted sensation (Se) attend more to sensory experience in the moment. This kind of deep analysis helps break through blind spots in self-knowledge and clarify the underlying logic of one's cognitive preferences and behavioral patterns.
- Developing strengths: the eight functions can precisely identify an individual's dominant and auxiliary functions, which are the areas of innate strength. Take the ENTP (dominant extraverted intuition, auxiliary introverted thinking): its powerful divergent thinking and capacity for logical argument are its core competencies. Once these strengths are clear, they can anchor a direction for career choice or personal growth, turning talent into real momentum for development.
- Improving weaknesses: the inferior and shadow functions in the Jungian eight-function model point to the blind spots in growth that an individual needs to break through. Someone who relies excessively on extraverted feeling (Fe), for example, may neglect the rational judgment of introverted thinking (Ti). With theoretical guidance, consciously developing weaker functions can effectively break the imbalance in personality development and achieve a more complete psychological integration.
- Psychological regulation: the eight functions reveal the mechanism of regression under stress—people tend unconsciously to call on their least mature function to cope with a crisis. Under high pressure, for example, an INFJ may rely excessively on extraverted sensation (Se) and lapse into blind impulsiveness. Understanding this pattern of reaction makes it possible to plan regulation strategies in advance and, by consciously calling on healthy functions, to keep one's psychological state stable and balanced.

### 4.2 Interpersonal relationships

- 
- Communication and understanding: by identifying another person's Jungian cognitive preferences, one can understand their thinking patterns and information-processing preferences in depth. People of the extraverted intuition (Ne) type, for example, are given to leaping associations, so it helps to leave room for divergent topics in conversation; introverted sensation (Si) types attend more to detail and experience, so communication with them calls for concrete examples and supporting data—measures that markedly improve the exchange.
- Conflict resolution: members of different Jungian types in a team differ noticeably in what they focus on and how they decide about the same problem. When conflict arises, analyzing the root of the two sides' cognitive differences through the eight-function theory—extraverted thinking (Te) pursuing efficiency and logic, for instance, while introverted feeling (Fi) cares more about inner values—and then reconciling the disagreement accordingly can effectively reduce the misunderstanding and conflict caused by cognitive bias.
- Teamwork: building a team on the basis of the Jungian eight-function theory allows members' strengths to complement one another.

### 4.3 Career development

- Career choice: finding work that suits one's cognitive preferences
- Career planning: clarifying the direction of one's career development
- Workplace adaptation: understanding patterns of interpersonal interaction at work

### 4.4 Psychotherapy

- Identifying psychological problems: understanding the deeper causes of psychological difficulties
- Designing treatment: creating individualized treatment plans based on personality type
- Supporting the individuation process: helping individuals achieve wholeness and harmony of personality

- Personality types develop and change as a person grows
- The theory should be applied with the aim of fostering personal growth and understanding, not of labeling people

## Conclusion

Jung's theory and the Jungian eight functions offer us a valuable framework for understanding the human psyche and personality. They not only help us know ourselves better but also let us understand others more deeply. What matters is that these theories should be used as tools for personal growth and mutual understanding, not as labels for rigid classification.

By studying and applying Jung's theory, we can move toward a more complete and harmonious development of personality and accomplish what Jung called the process of "individuation"—becoming truly ourselves.
